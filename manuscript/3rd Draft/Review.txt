This is a substantial pivot, not just a revision — worth naming clearly before getting into the checklist. You've dropped the DDPM/counterfactual generation pipeline entirely and replaced it with a classification-plus-attribution paper: the same anatomically-decomposed, territory-dropout SE-ResNet1D classifier, now paired with a three-tier attribution stack (macro occlusion sensitivity, meso Grad-CAM++, micro Integrated Gradients) instead of generative counterfactuals. That's a legitimate strategic move — it sidesteps essentially every issue I raised about the diffusion approach (the identity-preservation contradiction, circular flip validation, unvalidated synthetic waveforms) by not doing that anymore. But it's a different paper with its own new set of issues, so let me review it on its own terms.

What the pivot successfully resolves
No more circularity in the headline claim. Occlusion sensitivity, Grad-CAM++, and Integrated Gradients are all post-hoc attributions on the real patient signal — there's no generative model whose output quality depends on fooling the same classifier that built it. This structurally eliminates the biggest problem from the previous draft.
Statistical testing has appeared. A paired Wilcoxon test (Model 3 vs. Model 2, p<0.05) is a genuine improvement.
Evaluation protocol is more rigorous. True cyclic 10-fold rotation (train/val/test sets all rotate) is stronger than a single fixed 8/1/1 split.
New, useful engineering detail: parameter counts (Table 3, showing Model 3 uses 35.5% fewer parameters than the flat baseline while outperforming it — a nice, checkable claim), full architecture table, augmentation/regularization protocol, and a hyperparameter ablation for p_drop.
IG's completeness axiom is a real, checkable mathematical property, and reporting the empirical violation (0.0124 ± 0.004, under the 0.02 tolerance) is good practice.
New issues this version introduces

1. The attribution audit's sample sizes undermine its own headline claims. Table 5's per-class N is small — HYP has N = 4. A claim like "the network allocates 59.3% of attribution to the Lateral leads... adhering to Sokolow-Lyon criteria" built on four patients is not evidence a reviewer can weigh; it's an anecdote dressed as a statistic. CD (N=13) and STTC (N=36) aren't much better. This is structurally the same problem as the small-N clinical claims in the previous draft — it didn't go away, it moved.

2. The audit cohort (N≈224 total across classes) doesn't match the stated test cohort (N=2,198). The paper says the audit runs "across the held-out PTB-XL Fold 10 test cohort (N=2,198)" but Table 5's counts sum to 224. If this audit was restricted to high-confidence and/or correctly-classified cases, that's a reasonable design choice — but it's undisclosed, and it's also a serious selection-bias concern: filtering to confident-correct predictions before checking whether attribution "concordance with clinical anatomy" is close to guaranteeing the result you report. This needs to be stated explicitly, and ideally the audit should also be run on the full cohort (including misclassified/low-confidence cases) so a reviewer can see whether concordance holds or degrades outside the cherry-picked subset.

3. "Dominant attribution %" needs a defined ground truth. The claim that MI attribution splits 55%/31% between Antero-Septal/Inferior "cleanly mirrors the clinical incidence of LAD and RCA culprit lesions" is comparing your model's population-level attribution split to population-level epidemiological statistics about infarct location — but PTB-XL's MI label is a superclass without per-patient infarct-territory ground truth (LAD vs. RCA vs. LCx) as far as I'm aware. If that's correct, this comparison is two different quantities that happen to be in the same units, not a validated per-patient concordance check. Worth being explicit about exactly what ground truth (if any) each attribution claim is checked against.

4. "Hallucination Risk: Zero" (Table 1) is an overclaim. Post-hoc attribution methods have their own well-documented faithfulness failures — this is not a fringe concern, it's the subject of your own cited papers (Jain & Wallace on attention; Tonekaboni et al. and Ismail et al. on time-series attribution). The standard rigor check the field expects here — and which is conspicuously absent — is a sanity check / model-randomization test (Adebayo et al., "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps"): reinitialize the network's weights (or a subset of layers) and confirm the Grad-CAM++/IG maps change accordingly. Without that, "zero hallucination risk" and "axiomatic completeness" read as claims about mathematical form (which are true) being used to imply faithfulness (which hasn't been tested).

5. IG's zero baseline is a known weak point for ECG specifically — and your own cited work says so. A flatline signal isn't obviously "neutral" for a physiological waveform (an isoelectric segment is itself diagnostically meaningful). This exact critique is central to the time-series interpretability literature you cite ([8],[9]). Worth either justifying the zero baseline explicitly or reporting sensitivity to an alternative (e.g., a per-patient PR-segment baseline, or a blurred/noised baseline), since this is a natural reviewer question.

6. Possible cross-draft inconsistency worth double-checking before submission. This draft reports p_drop = 0.15 as optimal, with Model 3 achieving ROC-AUC 0.9329 [95% CI 0.9270–0.9388] and F1 0.7836 — numbers that are identical to the fourth decimal and identical CI bounds to the previous draft, which reported p_drop = 0.2 for the same result. Either this is coincidence from a shared random seed, or the dropout-rate change wasn't actually re-run before the metrics were copied forward. Worth verifying this is a real, re-executed result and not a stale figure, since a sharp-eyed reviewer comparing versions (or just checking your own ablation table, which claims p_drop≥0.30 degrades PTB-XL AUC to 0.9215) could raise this.

7. Citations I could not verify — please double-check before submission. I searched for "MLSA-Net" (Mehari & Strodthoff, IEEE TBME 2023) and "TransECG" (Niu et al., Biomedical Signal Processing and Control 2023), both cited as comparison baselines in Table 7, and could not locate either paper. Mehari and Strodthoff are real, active ECG researchers with a real, correctly-cited 2022 self-supervised paper elsewhere in your reference list ([17]) — but I found no trace of a "MLSA-Net" paper by them, and no trace of "TransECG" by Niu et al. This may simply be a search miss on my end, but incorrect author/venue attribution on a citation is a serious problem if it reaches review, so I'd treat this as a must-verify item, not a nice-to-have.

8. The cross-paper caveat got weaker, not stronger. The previous draft's Table 5 had an explicit footnote cautioning that cross-paper comparisons use different cohorts/protocols. This draft's Table 7 (comparing PTB-XL AUC and F1 against Strodthoff, Smigiel, MLSA-Net, TransECG) has no equivalent caveat, even though the same concern applies — F1 in particular is highly sensitive to averaging scheme and thresholding, which differs across papers.

Framing point worth thinking about strategically

By dropping the generative counterfactual angle, you've also dropped the paper's most novel contribution. Occlusion sensitivity, Grad-CAM++, and Integrated Gradients are all pre-existing, well-established methods (2017–2019); the genuine architectural contribution here (anatomical branching + territory dropout) is real but is now paired with a fairly conventional "apply three known XAI methods hierarchically" framing rather than a new generative paradigm. That's not fatal — hierarchical multi-tier attribution tied to anatomical structure is a reasonable framing, and it's much more defensible than the diffusion approach was — but reviewers at the very top venues may now see this as incremental (combination of known techniques) rather than novel, whereas the diffusion draft's issue was the opposite (novel but under-validated). It's a real trade-off, not a strict improvement, and worth being clear-eyed about which reviewer critique you'd rather defend against.

Updated verdict

This version is more defensible on rigor grounds than the diffusion draft, but it has swapped one class of problems (generative validity) for another (small-N attribution claims, an unstated selection filter in the audit cohort, an absolute claim that overreaches what post-hoc attribution can prove, and citations that need verification). It reads as solidly publishable at IEEE JBHI / Computers in Biology and Medicine tier with moderate fixes. For top-tier "AI + medicine" venues, the priority fixes are: (1) run the attribution audit on the full Fold-10 cohort, not a filtered high-confidence subset, and report how concordance changes; (2) either drop the N=4 HYP claim or explicitly flag it as illustrative, not statistical; (3) add a model-randomization sanity check for Grad-CAM++/IG; (4) soften "zero hallucination risk" and "mathematical proof" language throughout; (5) verify the MLSA-Net/TransECG citations; and (6) confirm the p_drop=0.15 result was actually re-run.
